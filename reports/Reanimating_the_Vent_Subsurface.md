# A Search for Life Where No One Has Looked: Reanimating the Vent Subsurface

**Daniel Moskal · Independent researcher · Chicago, IL · triboiclife@gmail.com**

*A supplement to "Life Powered Like Lightning" (Report A) and the Triboic Life Technical Companion (Report B), sharpening the deep-sea vent creature catalogued in The Triboic Frontier and in the sulfur adjunct. This is a pre-design proposal for review, not a finished protocol and not a result. Nothing here claims the creature exists.*

---

## The ask, first

The target is a diffuse vent field, one of the gentle sites where warm, oxygen-free seawater seeps steadily upward through cracked rock, and where the outflow sometimes pushes out snowflake-like masses of microbial matter. These are not the black smokers most people picture. A black smoker's fluid is extremely hot, and only its thin outer skin, where the vent fluid mixes with oxygenated seawater, ever reaches temperate levels. A diffuse field is different. There, temperate, anoxic seawater flows upward continuously, and it is that flow, not a scalding jet, that makes the place interesting.

I propose to take core samples of the shallow rock and sediment beneath a diffuse field, at the less costly option of not pressurizing the cores. I accept that the deepest, most pressure-committed organisms in them will die on the way to the surface. What survives, or reanimates, is the target. The cored material goes into a transparent pipe in a laboratory. Anoxic seawater runs over it at a constant flow for a few months. And then the work is simply to watch what wakes up: to reanimate single-celled organisms, and to see whether anything hatches from resting spores or eggs held in the rock.

That is the experiment.

It is highly speculative that triboic life would wake in a recreated laboratory version of these conditions. I want to be plain about that. But it is highly likely that previously unknown sulfur bacteria will reanimate, and that a partial map of an unexplored ecosystem, the reservoir beneath a diffuse vent field, will be drawn for the first time. The anoxic, flowing, temperate zone beneath a diffuse field is the most hospitable habitat on Earth for the theorized triboic vent creature, and that is what inspired this design. But the value does not depend on the triboic theory being right. Whatever one thinks of that theory, it is safe to expect that unknown species will be found in these cores, and that their ecosystem will be partially explained for the first time.

I lead with the ask, rather than the hypothesis, because the ask stands on its own. The subsurface reservoir under a diffuse vent field is one of the least-examined inhabited places on Earth. Simply seeing what reanimates from it is a genuine discovery, even if nothing exotic is ever found. The triboic question rides on top of that floor. It does not hold it up.

## Why this place, and why no one has been

A tribowhip needs three things from any home: liquid water, a steady flow to keep it fluttering, and enough chemistry in that water to help pay the bills. The vent creature adds a fourth condition, specific to this habitat: the water must be anoxic. The logic is unforgiving. Where there is oxygen, oxygen-breathing life is so efficient that it wins, and a slow triboic metabolism never gets a foothold. So the creature, if it exists, must live where oxygen does not: in the pure anoxic flow, past the thin mixing skin where vent fluid meets oxygenated seawater and where all the familiar vent animals crowd.

That anoxic flow below the sea floor has never been observed. Cameras stop at the surface of the vent and watch the fluid mix with cold seawater above it. But in a diffuse field, the snowflake-like masses of bacterial life that are pushed out come from below the rock. I propose to sample the fractured rock those blooms come from. The simplest first look at this zone is to take a core, bring it to the surface, and place it in a laboratory pipe under similar chemistry and flow.

## What is already known, and what is genuinely new

The experiment must be honest about how much of it is ordinary. Most of the pieces exist. The novelty is in how they are combined.

Reanimating dormant life is real, not wishful. Microbes pulled from subseafloor sediment more than a hundred million years old have been revived and made to multiply thousands-fold once fed [LAB, Morono et al. 2020, *Nature Communications* 11:3626]. That result is the anchor beneath the whole idea of waking a reservoir. But two cautions ride with it, and both matter. First, those revived cells were aerobic, oxygen-users from oxygen-bearing sediment, not the anaerobes an anoxic pipe would favor. The paper's own title records this: it is about aerobic microbial life in oxic sediment. Second, they were single cells, and a large fraction were not even spore-formers, not the resting eggs or spores of anything complex. So reanimation is on solid ground for single-celled life, and it is frank speculation for anything multicellular. The anchor is real, but it does not reach as far as the triboic question needs, and that gap is stated here rather than blurred.

Culturing vent microbes from sediment in synthetic seawater is likewise routine [LAB; citation needs verification for a specific reference]. So is keeping vent organisms alive at pressure, using expensive pressure-retaining samplers and incubators [LAB; citation needs verification for a specific reference]. Neither of those is the contribution here. I have deliberately chosen to sample unpressurized cores for this first experiment.

What is new is threefold. First, the aim is to reanimate a dormant reservoir under a diffuse field, rather than to culture an active community that was already growing. Second, the design imposes a sustained, controllable flow, the condition a triboic organism would actually need, and one that static culturing never provides. Third, the whole thing is treated as a search for triboic life, not only for microbes. The first two recombine known methods in a new way. The third is the reason for doing it.

One fact about the rock matters enough to pull out, because it decides whether the flow idea is even coherent. Seawater does not ooze evenly through vent rock like water through a sponge. It runs through cracks. The fractures, not the fine pore space, carry almost all the flow, and that flow is channelled into a few fast pathways [LAB, Fisher & Becker 2000, *Nature* 403:71–74]. This is good news for a fluttering whip. A whip anchored in a flowing fracture feels real force, while one buried in dead pore space feels almost nothing. It also tells us where to core: fractured rock, not massive basalt.

## The design

The design is simple, and its simplicity is the point. This is not a clever detector. It is a rack of pipes that rebuilds the vent world and waits for it to come back to life.

Each pipe is a clear tube, acrylic or glass, about a metre long and a hand's-breadth across, packed with cored material so that fluid is forced through the fractured rock, not merely past it. Anoxic seawater is pumped through in a closed loop at a constant flow and kept oxygen-free for the whole run. The medium is stripped of oxygen with an inert gas, the loop is built from materials air cannot seep through, and a sensor in the line confirms the seal holds. Every pipe flows, always. That is not a variable to test. It is the world being recreated, because flow is what the vent has and what the creature would need.

What differs from pipe to pipe is the recipe. Some pipes carry one chemistry, some another: different sulfur compounds, different dissolved gases, different trace nutrients, matched to what the reservoir might hold. And the flow runs faster in some pipes than others. These conditions are spread across the rack because there is no way to know in advance which recipe or which current a given sleeper needs to wake, so the design covers a range and lets the samples sort themselves out. Then all of it runs for a few months, and what happens is recorded.

That is the whole method. There is no separate triboic test bolted on, because none is needed. If triboic life is down there, it is down there as spores or eggs or resting cells, and it either hatches under these conditions or it does not. A creature that appears, something that grows into a body where the map allows only single cells, announces itself by existing. It does not have to be tricked into revealing itself. It only has to be given the world it needs, and watched.

Watching happens two ways, one cheap and constant, one occasional and deeper. The cheap way is to sample the water leaving each pipe: for cloudiness, for cell counts, for the chemical traces of metabolism, and for DNA and RNA that say who is present and who is actually alive and active. This runs continuously and never opens the loop. The deeper way is to look inside: a camera watching films and filaments spread across the clear wall, and, now and then, a medical or industrial scan of the packed rock to catch growth in its interior that never reaches a surface. No single method sees everything, so the design leans on the constant water-sampling for coverage and the imaging for the closer looks.

Before any of this, each core is split. One part is fixed and frozen at once, then examined under the microscope and by sequencing, to census everything that was there, living and dead, including the deep organisms that will not survive the trip up. The other part goes into the pipes to reanimate. The gap between the full census and the list of what actually wakes is itself a result. It records what was lost, so that "did not survive collection" is never mistaken for "was not there."

A first run is a modest rack: enough pipes to spread several chemistries across a couple of flow rates, with duplicates to tell a real signal from noise, which lands at something like a dozen to two dozen pipes. None of it is exotic hardware. It is aquarium engineering, anaerobic-culture technique, and ordinary imaging, combined in a way no one has combined them for this purpose.

## What it would, and would not, settle

The floor is firm. Cored reservoir rock almost certainly holds dormant life that will stir under restored anoxic flow, and characterizing it is real science whatever else happens. A blank result on the exotic question is still a full result on the ordinary one: a first map of an ecosystem no one has described. That is the discipline the whole project is built on. Design it so that finding nothing unusual still means finding something.

The triboic question comes with honest limits, and they should be stated plainly rather than buried. Letting the cores decompress means the deepest, most pressure-committed organisms, arguably the ones a subsurface triboic creature would most resemble, die before the pipe ever sees them [LAB, obligate piezophiles cannot survive decompression to surface pressure; Yayanos & Dietz 1982; reviewed in Merino et al. 2019, *Frontiers in Earth Science*]. So a blank result cannot rule triboic life out. It can only say that none of the survivors showed it. And the leap from "single cells revived" to "a resting egg or spore hatched into a body under anoxic flow" has no precedent. It stays [SPEC], and it must not borrow confidence from the well-anchored floor beneath it. What the experiment offers the triboic question is not proof, but the first fair chance: the most hospitable place that can be named, rebuilt where it can be watched.

There is a quieter prediction worth recording, because it turns a weakness into a design feature. If a triboic organism truly dies when the flow stops, its current cut and its power gone, then it must have a way to wait out the dead intervals, which at a vent are inevitable as cracks seal and fields wander. That way is a spore, a cyst, an egg: a dormant stage built to survive until flow returns. Which is to say the creature, if it exists, should be exactly the kind of thing a reanimation experiment is built to wake, and should look from the outside like the seedbank that feeds a snowblower bloom. This proves nothing, since ordinary microbes form resting stages too. But it means the experiment is aimed at the right life stage rather than against it.

## Two roads left for later

This first pass takes the cheap path on purpose, and two costlier roads are left open for when its results justify them.

The first is pressure. Because the cores are allowed to decompress, this experiment knowingly gives up the obligate high-pressure life, organisms that cannot survive at the surface at all, whose membranes and inner machinery are tuned to the deep and come apart when the pressure drops [LAB, obligate piezophile decompression sensitivity; Merino et al. 2019, *Frontiers in Earth Science*]. A later campaign that captures and holds cores at full pressure would reach exactly the life this one gives up, and it is the natural sequel once the surface experiment shows what is down there.

The second is the recharge zone, the other end of the vent's plumbing, where cold seawater is drawn down into the crust through fractures, loses its oxygen to the rock, and turns slowly acidic as it descends. It is anoxic in its deeper reaches, it flows through the same channelled cracks, and it carries a distinctive acid chemistry that ties into the wider project's interest in acid environments. Its fracture flow may suit a whip even better than a diffuse field's gentler seep. But it is colder, its habitable band is a narrow depth window, and finding an active recharge site is hard. It belongs on the map as future work, not in the first experiment.

Both roads share the same shape. This proposal is the reachable first step, and each of these is a costlier, deeper follow-on that the first step's findings would justify.

One line on why any of this reaches past Earth. Europa has long been suspected to hide hydrothermal vents like our own beneath its ice, though recent modeling has begun to question whether its seafloor is geologically active enough to sustain them [LAB for the long-standing suspicion of Europan vents, e.g. NASA Europa science; LAB for the recent challenge, Byrne et al. 2026, *Nature Communications*; SPEC for anything living in them]. That live scientific debate is part of the point. A method that can read dormant life out of vent rock, and read the habitability of a vent system, would carry directly to the places we most want to search next, whichever way the Europa question resolves. This experiment is built to be run on Earth, with Earth's tools, at an Earth vent. That it might also teach us how to look under an icy moon is a consequence, not the goal.
