# Life Powered Like Lightning

**Daniel Moskal**
*Independent researcher* · Chicago, IL · triboiclife@gmail.com


*As more exoplanets are discovered, we can't help but wonder if there is life like there is on Earth. But that question carries a hidden assumption: that the energy source for life must come from photons or from chemistry. What if an energy cycle could be powered partially or fully by electricity itself?*

As more exoplanets are discovered, we can't help but wonder if there is life like there is on Earth. But that question has limitations. It assumes the energy source for life would come from photons or chemistry. What if an energy cycle could be powered partially or fully by electricity itself? I am proposing a theoretical class of life, triboic life, which could use triboelectricity — capturing the friction created by moving air or liquid. Perhaps, in addition to photoautotrophs and chemoautotrophs, we can add triboautotrophs.

An idea this broad runs the risk of quickly losing focus. A deliberate choice was made to develop just one minimal creature, in one place, and to choose the energy-cycle chemistry with the most proven wet-chemistry benchmarks. So the idea was tested in the form of one creature, Fulgorax, on a Venus simulation, with carbon chemistry — to show both how to look for it and how honest a hypothesis has to be about its own limits.

The friction caused by fluid motion could power the creature with electricity to run its chemistry, so oxygen is not needed for complex life. On cold planets it might also provide friction heat, letting the creature raise its internal temperature to enable ideal life chemistry. This would mean that the idea of a habitable zone is null, radically changing how we view the search for life.

Triboic life runs the risk of invoking emotion at the expense of scientific inquiry. I call this the beautiful-theory theorem — that a theory can be so beautiful that it must be true. Of course, this can easily blind objectivity. At the same time, others may feel an emotional repulsion to triboic life, because if the habitable-zone hypothesis is nullified, the search for life loses clarity. This is why the exploration of triboic life, and this report, spent considerable effort trying to disprove the idea and clearly labeling when ideas are speculative. The hope is that this paper will begin a discussion of the many adaptations this theoretical triboautotroph could make to many environments, and inspire research into the many wet-lab benchmarks that are needed but have not yet been established.

You already know triboelectricity, even if not by that name. It is the spark that jumps to your finger at a doorknob in winter, the crackle when you pull off a wool hat, the force that pins a balloon to your hair. It is lightning, on the grandest scale. We usually call it static electricity, the charge that builds when two materials touch and separate. Triboelectricity is its proper scientific name, and it is one of the oldest and most ordinary effects in physics, present anywhere two surfaces meet and part.

Now imagine harnessing it deliberately. Engineers already do: a triboelectric nanogenerator (TENG) is a small sealed ribbon of stacked material layers that press together and pull apart as the ribbon flexes, pumping out a pulse of current with every cycle. Set such a ribbon fluttering in a moving fluid — wind or liquid current — and the flow itself becomes the thing that flexes it, stroke after stroke, powered by the environment. Call the result a tribowhip: a sealed, layered ribbon that turns the motion of a fluid into a trickle of electricity, its energy-producing layers contained in a hardened skin which blocks out the weather and chemistry beyond. The charge is generated entirely within. A living thing built around such whips could, in principle, take many forms — different bodies, different chemistries, different worlds. To keep this article grounded we will commit to just one, and give it a name: Fulgorax, a millimetres-wide drifter designed so that every step of its chemistry stays in the best-studied territory we have. One concrete example, built carefully, is worth more than a gallery of vague possibilities.

The question this article follows is simple to ask and hard to answer: could life learn to live on triboelectricity? This is an accounting challenge. Does the tribowhip create more energy than it takes to build and maintain the tribowhip?

Every living thing needs a steady supply of energy to power its engine, repair itself against the slow pull of decay, and build the next generation. On Earth, that energy comes from sunlight or from chemistry: photosynthesis and chemosynthesis are the two best-known engines biology runs on. Electricity as a metabolic energy source is not entirely without precedent — a growing astrobiology literature on electroautotrophs describes microbes that pull electrons directly from minerals or electrodes. But those organisms draw their current from an external donor in the environment. What is proposed here is different: a creature that generates its own charge internally, in a sealed structure that touches nothing, driven only by the flutter of the fluid around it. A flutter in a fast wind is a genuine source of usable energy. If a living structure could capture it and power the chemistry of life, then a distinct kind of metabolism becomes thinkable.

The idea's name is triboic life, after the triboelectric effect that would power it. The definition is deliberately narrow. It is a living system that harvests electrical charge from its own fluttering parts, the tribowhips, driven by the flow of the fluid it lives in. The criterion is just as narrow, and it is the whole game. The energy the whips bring in must pay for itself. Building a whip costs energy; repairing it as the environment wears it down costs more. Unless the catch exceeds the rent, the idea collapses. Everything that follows is an accounting question, and a dispassionate reading of the accounting ledger is key to any meaningful development of the triboic life idea.

What makes the idea worth taking seriously is that it is not fussy about where it happens. The mechanism needs only a flexible structure and a fast, sustained flow. Those conditions are met in a great many places that have nothing else in common: a turbulent atmosphere, a fast current beneath an ice shell, anywhere a fluid consistently drives a surface to flutter. Triboic life, if it is possible at all, is not a story about one planet. It is a story about a type of living thing, defined by how it makes its living rather than by where.

And it need not make its whole living this way. Triboelectric current could be the primary power source for a simple organism. It could also be a supplement, a top-up bolted onto an otherwise familiar metabolism. Imagine a creature that runs mostly on weak sunlight or on a sluggish chemical reaction, with triboic electricity providing the boost needed for bigger and more vigorous life. In that role the flutter is not the engine but the booster — the increment that turns a metabolism that almost closes into one that does. This flexibility, the ability to serve as either the engine or the boost across very different chemistries, is what makes triboic life a class rather than a single hypothesis.

A class of life defined this abstractly is easy to propose and hard to pin down. So the rest of this article does something specific with it: it takes that one carefully built creature and tests it somewhere real. Fulgorax is carbon-based, using triboelectricity alone to power itself. This is the simplest design, and, most importantly, carbon-based chemistry is the most studied and benchmarked. Then we put it in a place where the idea could be tested: a Venus simulation, because we are sending probes to Venus soon and can measure part of its atmosphere from orbit now. Venus is close, it is well studied, spacecraft are on their way, and its clouds are something we can actually go looking through. But it is a sulfur world, and there are anomalies in the Venusian clouds tied to sulfur chemistry — and the wet-lab benchmarks involving highly concentrated sulfuric acid and electricity are mostly absent. What happens when the idea meets that real, hostile, well-studied atmosphere turns out to be more interesting than a simple pass or fail.

## Why Venus, and the rules of the test

An abstract idea can absorb any amount of argument without ever being settled; the only cure is to make it concrete enough to be wrong. So the Fulgorax creature was built with a specific home in mind, and Venus was the choice. The reasons have less to do with Venus being a likely place for Fulgorax to live than with it being a place where the idea can be checked.

Venus is close. It is already among the most thoroughly studied bodies in the solar system, and after decades of neglect it is about to get a great deal more attention. A fleet of spacecraft is being readied to fly there within the next several years, some of them designed to fly through the clouds and taste what they are made of. An exoplanet, by contrast, is a smear of light across light-years. Its gross atmospheric chemistry can be inferred at best, and nothing can be sent to sample it. Venus offers a rare combination: a genuinely extreme environment, with persistent wind to power the tribowhips, and an atmosphere an instrument can be flown directly into.

That decision drove the next one, and it is worth being candid about it. The creature's metabolism was not chosen to be the chemistry most likely to suit Venus. It was chosen to be the chemistry that could be modeled and defended. Life on Venus, if it exists, might well run on sulfur. The planet's clouds are saturated with sulfur compounds, and sulfur-based metabolisms are chemically reasonable there. But sulfur bioenergetics is sparsely benchmarked, which would have meant reasoning with few anchors and a great deal of guesswork. Carbon chemistry is the opposite. Carbon-based metabolisms are the most heavily studied in all of biology. Their energy costs, their intermediates, and their isotopic fingerprints are measured and tabulated. So Fulgorax was deliberately given a carbon-based, oxygen-free metabolism, knowing it might not be the most natural fit for Venus, precisely because every step could be checked against laboratory data rather than asserted. It is the choice built to limit speculation, not the choice built to explain Venusian atmospheric anomalies. The difference between those two becomes the heart of the story later.

That choice carries a cost the creature has to pay in its own body. An oxygen-free world offers no convenient oxygen to absorb the spent electrons every metabolism must shed. Fulgorax has to supply that electron acceptor itself, manufacturing and regenerating the molecule that closes its electrical circuit. It is a demanding way to live, and the design was held to it.

One rule governed everything that followed. The reasoning ran strictly forward: from the creature's chemistry to its waste products, from those products to the traces they would leave in the air, and only then to what an instrument might detect. It never ran backward from a hoped-for result. The aim was never to make Venus's data fit the creature. It was to predict, honestly and in advance, what this particular creature would do to the air around it, and then see how that prediction fared.

## The creature, and its knife-edge

Picture a fluffball about five millimetres across, half the width of a pea, drifting in the wind. Almost all of that span is tribowhips. Roughly sixteen hundred of them, each a millimetre or two long, radiate outward from a small central body that is itself well under a millimetre across. The body is the living core, the part that holds the cell machinery. The tribowhips are its forest of antennae, and they are most of what an observer would see. Fulgorax does not fly or swim under its own power. The seventy-metre-per-second winds of Venus move the tribowhips relative to its body, and its hard sporopollenin surface protects the tribowhips from the acid droplets that strike them intermittently.

Each tribowhip is the engine, and its design is the part the project worked hardest to pin down. It is a sealed ribbon of four layers, and only one boundary inside it produces electricity. The charge is generated where two of the layers meet: a soft charge-carrying skin, most likely a cellulose derivative, pressed against a gel core laced with quinones. As the wind flexes the ribbon, those two surfaces inside the sealed whip touch and separate, touch and separate, and with each cycle a pulse of charge crosses between them.

Because that interface is sealed away inside the whip, the charge it makes never touches the outside world. This sounds like a technicality. It is the reason the whole creature is possible. The obvious objection to any static-electric device is that its surroundings should bleed the charge away, and Venus's clouds carry concentrated sulfuric acid droplets, about as charge-hungry a thing as exists. But a droplet cannot drain a charge it never contacts. The acid's job is only to flutter and strike the whip from the outside. The electricity stays sealed within, untouched by whatever the whip is drifting through.

That single interface is the generator. The other two layers earn their place by keeping it alive: an outer coat of sporopollenin, the same tough material that armours pollen grains on Earth, here doubling as acid shield and ultraviolet sunscreen; and a layer of silk that takes the bending and the battering so the delicate generating pair does not have to. Silk's job is structural, not electrical. A 2023 survey of biopolymer charging behaviour by Hanlu Meng and colleagues placed silk on the wrong side of the electrical ledger to serve as the charge-maker, which is why the cellulose skin, not the silk, is paired against the gel.¹

It is worth being clear about what this is not. The triboelectric generators engineers build for real power usually stack the same charge-making pair over and over, dozens of layers wired together, each one adding to the current. Fulgorax does nothing of the kind. It carries exactly one generating pair, wrapped in the two layers it cannot survive without — the fewest working layers a triboic whip can have. That spareness is deliberate; a minimal design is the most sensible first design. A larger or hungrier organism could multiply its output by repeating the pair, but every extra layer is extra mass to build and to repair. Fulgorax is designed for minimal parts — less to be built and repaired. The extra complexity of a larger creature with more powerful tribowhips is left for future speculation.

In an atmosphere with no free oxygen to absorb the electrons every metabolism must shed, the quinone gel is the creature's electron acceptor. It is the molecule that catches each spent electron and hands it onward, then regenerates to do it again. The creature has to build and maintain that machinery itself, continuously, out of the same energy budget that builds the tribowhips. Which brings us to the only question that ever mattered.

Does the accounting close? The full ledger was built and run: every joule the whips bring in, against every joule spent building, repairing, anchoring, and smoothing their ragged pulses into usable current. The creature's upkeep was benchmarked against the leanest known living thing — the maintenance current that the microbiologist Dianne Newman's group has measured in bacteria idling at the very floor of life's energy budget.² The full arithmetic, with every assumption laid bare, lives in the companion technical report.

The verdict is what gives this section its name. At cautious assumptions the creature runs at a slight loss, and it flips to a surplus the moment it is allowed whips that wear more slowly, are built more hollow, and leak a little less charge. The speculative nature of describing a theoretical creature that might exist somewhere allows an uncomfortable amount of room to adjust the assumptions and reach more exciting results. But it is better to hold to the stingiest assumptions and see where the math takes you — which, in the case of Fulgorax living in the Venusian atmosphere, is a place where slightly more energy is needed than is produced.

This is what living on a knife's edge means: there is no comfort margin. The creature is not robustly alive with energy to spare, nor cleanly impossible. It sits within a hair of the break-even line, tipping to one side or the other on exactly the assumptions that are least certain. Triboic life, on this design, can exist only by getting nearly everything right at once.

There is a deeper consequence buried in that tribowhip count: the thousand-odd tribowhips are not decorative. They are a current requirement. Running the creature's basic upkeep, the maintenance chemistry that keeps it intact, turns out to be cheap — a job a single whip could power. But growing, building new body and fixing carbon out of the air, demands about a thousand times more electrical current. The only way to supply that is to run a thousand or more whips at once, their outputs combined in parallel like cells in a battery. A creature that merely wanted to persist might get away with very few whips. A creature that wants to reproduce needs the whole forest.

And a thousand whips is far too much hardware to hang on a single cell. The body has to be a cooperative aggregate of many cells — the minimum somewhere in the range of a few to a few dozen — sharing the load simply to afford the current that growth requires. The exact ceiling on how many whips one cell could carry is reasoned from the creature's geometry rather than calculated from first principles, another area needing further exploration. But the direction is clear: one cell is not enough.

This is a demanding conclusion, because it means triboic life of this kind cannot start simple. Most origin-of-life thinking leans on a single cell that later evolves into a multicellular creature. Here, the very first viable organism would already have to be multicellular in order to produce electricity. So Fulgorax would likely need to evolve its triboelectric machinery from an existing multicellular creature on its planet, or arrive via panspermia.

And that marginality is not a flaw in the story. It is the story. A creature balanced this finely is exactly the kind of test case worth running against reality, because the way it failed told us more than any comfortable success could have.

## Testing Fulgorax against Venus

Instead of asking whether life on Venus can explain the sulfur anomaly, a narrower question was asked. If this particular creature lived in those clouds, what would it do to the air around it, and could any instrument tell the difference between its handiwork and ordinary chemistry? Follow its chemistry forward: what its tribowhips shed as they wear, what its metabolism leaks, what it excretes. Each of those products mixes into the known chemistry of the cloud deck and becomes, in principle, a trace a probe could catch. The whole list was worked out.

That list was then sorted by a single brutal question: could this trace ever be told apart from non-biological chemistry? For most of the list, the answer was no. The creature would shed a reddish organic tar, but Venus almost certainly makes a near-identical "red oil" with no help from biology. The chemist Jan Spacek has shown how ordinary Venus-cloud chemistry produces just such conjugated organics.³ The creature would release carbon monoxide, but the upper atmosphere is already full of photochemical carbon monoxide that would swamp its trickle. It would leave elemental sulfur, but Venus has an abiotic sulfur cycle that makes elemental sulfur anyway. Most of what the creature does, the planet already does by itself. Find any one of those traces, and you have learned nothing.

Only two kinds of signal survived the cut. The first is a set of specific biological molecules with no known non-living source on Venus: organic nitrogen locked inside complex molecules, the discrete molecular masses of nucleobases, and the redox-active quinones the creature builds into its whips. These are molecular signatures rather than smudges. The premise underneath this — that such delicate molecules could even survive acid exposure — is not idle. A sustained laboratory program led by the MIT planetary scientist Sara Seager has shown nucleic-acid bases and other biomolecules holding up in concentrated sulfuric acid for months on end.⁴ That matters here because parts of the creature take intermittent droplet strikes and must tolerate the acid on contact — not because the creature is submerged in it.

The second surviving signal is subtler, and it became the most interesting thing the project found.

It is a matter of isotopes — the slightly heavier and lighter versions of the same atom. Living things on Earth are picky. Their chemistry tends to favor the lighter isotope of an element, leaving the products measurably enriched in the light version and the leftovers in the heavy. That preference is a recognized fingerprint of metabolism. And when the creature's three main jobs were worked forward — growing, by building its body from carbon; maintaining itself, by handling sulfur; and feeding, by cycling nitrogen from other organic matter — each one independently pointed to the same kind of signature: a tell-tale lightening of the carbon, the sulfur, the nitrogen. That convergence was not designed in. It emerged: three separate metabolic routes arriving at one class of measurement.

Two of those three legs are strong. The third is not, and the difference matters. The carbon and sulfur fingerprints rest on well-studied biochemistry. The nitrogen one is more speculative, and, awkwardly, it is the one no probe headed to Venus could even measure. So the convergence is real but not uniform: a sturdy two-legged result with a third leg that is more hope than evidence.

What makes the carbon and sulfur legs worth dwelling on is that they are abundance-independent. There is no way to know how many of these creatures a Venus cloud could support, since the honest range spans a factor of a hundred thousand, so any prediction that depends on how much of something is in the air is nearly worthless. An isotope ratio sidesteps that entirely. It is a property of the chemistry, not the headcount. One creature or a billion, the fingerprint reads the same.

And here is the part that turns the result from interesting to actionable. NASA's DAVINCI probe, due to drop through the Venusian clouds toward the end of this decade, carries a laser instrument built to measure exactly these isotope ratios for carbon- and sulfur-bearing gases, to a precision of about one part per thousand.⁵ That is the quiet payoff. A living metabolism would skew these isotopes by tens of parts per thousand, so the instrument is already several times sharper than the fingerprint it would be hunting. Two of the three convergent signals fall inside the reach of an instrument that is built and on its way. That is the real near-term yield of this whole exercise. It is not a claim that Venus is inhabited, but a specific, prioritized shopping list for the probes about to arrive: measure the carbon and sulfur isotopes, because that is where an answer — either way — actually lives.

So the test was run. And then it did something that was not scripted.

Venus has a genuine, decades-old anomaly in its sulfur chemistry, one of the real mysteries that make the planet such a puzzle. It sits high in the haze, well above where Fulgorax would live. The drifting fuzzball belongs in the temperate band of the cloud deck, around fifty kilometres up, where the pressure and temperature are almost Earth-like. The anomaly is some twenty kilometres higher still, in the cold upper haze. There, as the Belgian planetary scientist Ann Carine Vandaele and her colleagues mapped in detail, the amount of sulfur dioxide stops falling with altitude and starts rising again — an inversion that should not be there.⁶ To produce it, some unidentified reservoir must be feeding sulfur back into the upper haze, and no photochemical model has ever satisfactorily explained where it comes from. If anything on Venus were going to hint at an exotic chemistry at work, this is the kind of thing it would look like.

Fulgorax cannot explain it. And the way it fails is the most instructive result in this article.

Remember the deliberate choice from the start: a carbon creature, because carbon biochemistry is what there is data to defend. Sulfur, for this creature, produces only a thin trickle of elemental sulfur — in the wrong place and the wrong quantity to account for a planet-scale inversion tens of kilometres overhead. The creature that could most be defended on paper is the creature least able to say anything about the one sulfur puzzle Venus is actually presenting. The diagnosis is hard to escape.

That is not the failure it might look like. A creature quietly tuned to "explain" Venus would be a weaker piece of science, not a stronger one, because it would mean the biology had been reverse-engineered to fit the answer. This one was built forward, on its own logic, and then broke against Venus in a specific, legible way. And a specific failure, unlike a vague success, points somewhere. It points directly at the sulfur just set aside.

## What the failure points to

Step back from the specific creature and look at what its failure actually demonstrated. The chemistry it could not explain was sulfur chemistry, the one anomaly Venus is genuinely flaunting. And the reason it could not is that Fulgorax was built, on purpose, around carbon. Reverse that choice, and the outline of a better hypothesis appears in the gap the carbon creature left behind.

Imagine a triboic creature whose primary business is sulfur — one that earns its living running the sulfur reactions Venus's atmosphere is visibly doing, with triboelectric current as the supplement that pushes an otherwise marginal sulfur metabolism into the black. Such a creature would sit inside Venus's real chemistry rather than beside it, and its waste would be sulfur compounds in the thick of the very cycle that puzzles us. It is at least possible that a creature like that could account for what the carbon creature cannot. Another unexplored possibility is that the tribowhips contribute friction heat, which would let it live higher in the Venusian atmosphere, where the sulfur anomaly is.

The status of the triboic life idea, after testing a carbon-cycle-based Fulgorax in the Venusian atmosphere, should be clear: it is a direction, not a result. Whether a sulfur-based triboic metabolism can be drawn up with an honest, positive energy budget — the same brutal accounting Fulgorax barely survived — is a question this report did not attempt to answer. Understanding the chemistry of a hypothesized sulfur-based triboic life on Venus would require extensive wet-lab work on highly concentrated sulfuric acid and electricity mixed together. Part of the purpose of this report is to help identify where further chemistry benchmarks need to be developed. The sulfur-based triboic creature was not built. The work only found the creature-shaped hole where it might go.

And that is the real lesson of the whole exercise, the part that outlives the Venus test. Triboic life was never a hypothesis about one organism or one planet. It is a hypothesis about a mechanism — a way of turning the motion of a fluid into usable electric current — and the mechanism is portable. Fulgorax was one instance of it, deliberately the most defensible one available, built to be tested rather than to be likely. Its specific failure does not touch the general idea; if anything it sharpens it. The same triboelectric supplement that could not rescue a carbon metabolism on Venus might comfortably underwrite a sulfur one there, or a different metabolism somewhere else entirely: beneath the ice of an ocean moon, or in the gales of some far exoplanet. Triboic life lives off motion; anywhere motion is constant, triboic life could potentially live. And recall why the mechanism travels so freely. The generator is sealed, so it works the same whether the fluid fluttering it is sulfuric acid, ocean brine, or liquid methane. None of them can drain a charge they never touch. The booster from the opening of this article is the durable idea. The engine it boosts can change from world to world.

That portability carries a consequence worth saying plainly, because it cuts against a habit in the search for life. We tend to hunt for oxygen, to treat an atmosphere with no oxygen in it as a poor prospect, because it is assumed life needs an oxidizing environment to power complex life. But a triboic supplement is a way of paying for the expensive steps of a metabolism without a convenient oxygen supply to draw on. So if triboic life is possible at all, then an atmosphere without oxygen is not the dead end it looks like, and a planet need not show oxygen for us to count it a candidate worth examining. This is said carefully, as a possibility and not a finding.

None of this escapes two hard limits, and honesty requires naming them rather than letting the widening view paper over them.

The first and deepest: nothing in this work shows that any of this can actually be built. Fulgorax's entire inventory — tribowhips, gel, and protective coat — would have to be synthesized by a living cell from the meager feedstock the Venusian air provides: overwhelmingly CO₂ and N₂, with trace dust and water available only as sparse, concentrated-acid droplets. The creature drifts in that gas, not in a bath of acid, but it must both build its whole body from those thin materials and withstand acid on contact when droplets strike. No known chemistry does the building. Acid-survival is one thing, and the laboratory evidence for it is real. Synthesis from so poor a feedstock is another, and it is unproven. The whole edifice rests on that single wet-lab result, which nobody has produced.

The second limit is quieter. There is no way to say how many such creatures a Venus cloud could hold, since the honest range runs across five orders of magnitude, so this work predicts the chemical character a triboic biosphere would leave behind, never its quantity. Every signal it leans on was chosen to survive that ignorance. An isotope ratio reads the same whether the clouds hold one creature or a billion. The companion technical report, Report B, walks through these limits, and three more, in full.

So what survives the test is not a Venusian, and certainly not a discovery. What survives is a sharper, more portable hypothesis than the one this work started with, and a short list of measurements that could begin to test it. That is exactly what a perspective like this is for.

## What I left for others

Every honest hypothesis comes with a map of its own edges, and this one has a particularly clear edge. The most tractable version of triboic life was pursued — carbon-based, with triboelectricity as the primary engine — precisely because it could be checked against real chemistry at nearly every step. That choice left a more tantalizing version untouched, and it is worth describing, because it is exactly the kind of work this article hopes to provoke.

The original triboic intuition was not the one that got developed. I imagined a creature that would have the energy to build complex structures despite lacking access to energetic, oxygen-based chemistry. What if the electricity came from an organic triboelectric nanogenerator that supplemented the weak energy from chemistry, or the weaker photons from a red dwarf star? Fulgorax was designed assuming only triboelectricity would power it. Perhaps the knife-edge result from Fulgorax suggests triboic life would prefer to bundle electric current with another power source.

Such a creature might be built something like *Chlorella vulgaris* or *Auxenochlorella protothecoides* — light-absorbing pigments under a protective sporopollenin exterior. But this design can't merely be plausible; it has to be stronger than the arguments for an abiotic explanation. The absorber it would purport to explain already has a credible non-living account. In the laboratory, iron and sulfur minerals dissolved in sulfuric acid reproduce the mysterious absorption band rather well. A 2024 experiment by Clancy Zhijian Jiang and colleagues matched both ultraviolet and blue features with iron-sulfur chemistry, and a 2025 measurement of ferric chloride under Venus-like conditions tightened the match further.⁷ A biological explanation does not merely need to be possible. It has to outcompete an inorganic one that has already been demonstrated to work.

Whether this sulfur-energy creature could build itself has not been explored; the basic wet-chemistry experiments needed to answer that have not been done. Like Fulgorax, it would have to assemble itself from what the Venusian atmosphere offers — CO₂, N₂, dust, and the sparse water that exists there as highly concentrated sulfuric acid.

And that points to the larger invitation this article means to extend. Fulgorax is one cramped instance of a much broader idea, deliberately built to be testable rather than to be likely, and it deserves to be bettered, or broken. The richer questions are wide open. What would a sulfur-based triboic creature look like — the one the Venus test seemed to call for? What about triboic life in utterly different settings: the churning hydrogen of a gas giant, the deep atmosphere of an ice giant, fast-moving methane rivers, the dark ocean beneath an ice shell — anywhere a fluid moves hard enough to flex a living ribbon? And what of larger creatures than this half-centimetre drifter, organisms that could afford to stack their generators and live with a comfortable energy margin instead of on a knife's edge? And what about the heterotrophs that might feed on these tribotrophs?

This work figured out one small, marginal example in one hostile place. A companion catalogue, *The Triboic Frontier*, sketches dozens of other places the same mechanism might reach — other worlds, other fluids, other chemistries — as an open invitation rather than a finished account. Let others draw the bolder ones, test them, criticize this one, and build past it. A hypothesis like this earns its keep not by being right, but by being specific enough that someone else can take the next honest step.
